## Monte Carlo labels estimate future solvability

The authors create about 500K query/answer pairs by sampling 6–8 solutions per MATH problem from Qwen2-Math and Qwen2.5-Math models (7B/72B). They split solutions at blank lines into steps, then sample eight continuations from each step using a matching Qwen2.5-Math model. A hard positive label means at least one continuation reaches the correct final answer; if none do, the step is negative. A soft label is the fraction of successful continuations (§2.1).

This Monte Carlo (MC) label estimates whether the model can continue from that prefix to an answer, not whether the current mathematical step is intrinsically correct. A wrong step can be repaired later, creating an apparent positive; a correct step can be followed by a failed continuation, creating an apparent negative. Steps after the first zero-labeled step are dropped. The paper uses cross-entropy on the final token for hard labels and MSE for soft labels in v2. The eight-sample threshold is a design choice of this dataset, not a calibrated universal cutoff.

## Evaluation changes the conclusion

On the same Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct policy and best-of-8 over seven math benchmarks, average final-answer accuracy is 65.9 for MC-labeled 860K examples, 65.3 for judge-labeled 860K, 64.9 for human-labeled PRM800K 264K, and 66.2 for majority@8. This metric does not show a clear gain from MC process labels. On ProcessBench, however, first-error localization mean F1 is 40.1 for MC labels, 46.5 for judge labels, and 56.5 for human labels (v2 Tables 3–4). The result depends on whether the target is finding the first bad step or selecting a solution with a correct final answer.

The authors keep about 40% of the MC set after filtering for agreement with the judge; ProcessBench performance approaches judge supervision with fewer labels. A separate 3M-sample experiment filters to 1.5M, where hard labels outperform soft labels on the two reported metrics. These are math-specific label-efficiency results; they do not establish that a model judge is ground truth.

PRM-7B and PRM-72B reach best-of-8 averages of 67.6 and 69.3, compared with 66.2 majority@8 and 68.9 for a 72B outcome reward model. These are small margins in the specified math setup, not general proof that PRMs dominate ORMs. On a selected subset with a correct answer but flawed reasoning, many comparison PRMs detect the error in fewer than half of cases; the reported value is detection accuracy on that subset, not a false-positive rate (v2 Table 5).

## Limits

The paper studies mathematical reasoning, PRM label construction, and step-error localization. It does not report calibration metrics such as ECE or reliability curves, nor establish best practices for using PRMs inside RL. The evaluation's judge and generated-continuation labels both add their own error modes. Findings should not be generalized to code, open-domain agents, or faithful reasoning beyond the tested benchmarks.

**Reading takeaway:** a process reward can be trained from human steps, a judge, or continuation success, but these signals target different properties. Final-answer best-of-N and first-error localization can rank the same label sources differently.
