## Where trajectories and labels came from

The study uses a fixed model generator to sample solutions, then human annotators label each solution step as positive, negative, or neutral (§2.3–2.4). Positive means a step is correct/reasonable and advances the solution; neutral means it is reasonable and correct but does not advance it; negative marks an incorrect or unreasonable step. Annotators see the ground-truth final answer but not a reference solution (Appendix D).

The large-scale PRM800K collection has about 800K usable labels over 75K solutions to 12K MATH problems after quality filtering; the raw collection has more labels and samples (Appendix B). In Phase 2, the current PRM actively selects high-scoring solutions with wrong final answers for human annotation across ten generations. This is PRM-guided sample selection, **not MCTS trace generation**. The fixed generator samples candidate solutions; PRM scores help decide which errors annotators inspect.

## What the PRM/ORM comparison measures

For the large-scale result, candidate solutions are ranked by the product of their step scores and evaluated by whether the selected final answer is correct. On 500 held-out MATH test problems, best-of-1860 is 78.2% for PRM, 72.4% for ORM, and 69.6% for majority vote (§3, Figure 3). This is a solution-selection outcome, not a direct estimate that every step score correctly localizes an error.

The large-scale comparison is not a clean supervision-only ablation: the ORM is trained on roughly 100 uniformly sampled solutions per problem and a dataset about an order of magnitude larger than PRM800K, while PRM labels are actively selected and skew toward wrong-answer solutions (§3). Figure 4 adds a more controlled small-scale comparison using the same sampled solution sets, with supervision type changed; its process labels are generated by a larger PRM, so they are not an independent human-label oracle (§4.1).

The paper also reports an out-of-distribution check on 224 recent physics, calculus, chemistry, and contest-math problems: best-of-100 accuracy is 72.9% for PRM, 63.8% for ORM, and 61.3% for majority vote (Table 1). This is limited math/STEM transfer, not general reasoning or agent reliability. The authors acknowledge possible MATH pretraining contamination; comparative ordering may be less affected, but the paper does not prove the evaluations contamination-free (§6.3).

**Reading takeaway:** PRM800K shows that step labels can improve selection of correct math solutions in its setup. It does not establish a universal PRM recipe, direct stepwise truth, or that PRM training generally uses tree search. Human step labels, PRM-selected sampling, and the best-of-N answer metric are separate parts of the evidence.
