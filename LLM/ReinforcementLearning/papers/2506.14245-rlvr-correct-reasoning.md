## The mechanism is conditional

The paper analyzes answer-only binary reward under a “logic prior”: if a correct chain of thought is more likely to yield a correct answer than an incorrect CoT, then `α=P(answer correct | CoT correct) > β=P(answer correct | CoT incorrect)`. With nonzero reward variance and a sufficiently large GRPO group, the paper derives positive expected normalized advantage for correct-CoT samples and negative advantage for incorrect-CoT samples (§4, Theorem 1).

This explains a possible implicit selection effect; it is not a theorem that every model/task satisfies `α > β`, that every GRPO recipe monotonically improves reasoning, or that gains generalize. The authors note that pretrained biases can let an incorrect CoT reach the right answer, causing answer reward to reinforce it. Degenerate all-correct/all-wrong groups also have no within-group reward variance.

## CoT-Pass@K separates answer luck from judged reasoning

Ordinary Pass@K counts a correct final answer even if its CoT is flawed. The paper defines CoT-Pass@K to require both a correct answer and a judge-approved CoT; math chains are checked three times with any/all/majority voting variants (§3.1, Figure 2). The judge is DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B; repeated calls to one judge are not independent ground truth.

The core math comparison is Qwen2.5-32B base vs DAPO-Qwen-32B. DAPO retains a CoT-Pass@K advantage on AIME 2024/2025 through K=1024, while ordinary Pass@K lets the base catch up or exceed it. Results are weaker on MATH-500 and AMC23, there is no MinervaMath gain, and the authors cannot distinguish difficulty from contamination for some sets (§3.1, Figure 2). In the authors' DAPO reproduction, answer correctness approaches saturation while median judged-CoT correctness conditioned on a correct answer remains near 0.7; binary answer reward may stop providing learning signal for flawed reasoning (§5, Figure 5).

The evidence is model- and task-specific. The paper uses judge-based CoT labels, reports counterexamples and weak datasets, and compares post-trained checkpoints whose recipes differ beyond one isolated RL intervention. Its theory supplies a mechanism under assumptions; it does not replace external reasoning evaluation.

**Reading takeaway:** answer-only RLVR can favor correct reasoning when correct CoTs are more predictive of success, but answer reward is not a step-level truth signal. Evaluations should distinguish correct final answers, judged-correct reasoning, and generalization outside the optimized verifier/tasks.
